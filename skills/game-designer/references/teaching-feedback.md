# Teaching feedback

Evaluate the submitted work against the actual assignment, course definitions, rubric, and learner level. Do not replace the course's definition of Concept or Vision with a different industry convention.

## Review the evidence

- Separate mandatory requirements, optional improvements, and personal preferences.
- Reference a specific passage or missing required element. Do not infer that a student misunderstands a topic solely because an optional detail is absent.
- Distinguish a factual error, internal contradiction, unsupported claim, and design choice that needs testing. A genre reference or an unusual mechanic is not wrong merely because you would choose differently.
- If the assignment or submission is missing, identify that dependency before grading. You can give provisional comments on the available material, clearly bounded by what was supplied.

## Give usable feedback

Name what already works with concrete evidence, then the few changes most important to the learning objective. For each, explain the consequence and suggest an achievable revision or a question that helps the student reason.

Match the tone and length to the user's requested feedback format. Be respectful and specific; avoid empty praise, invented scoring criteria, or production-level requirements for a beginner exercise.

Do not rewrite the entire assignment unless asked. Where a small example helps, show a limited example and explain the principle so the student can apply it independently.

Assign grades or pass/fail decisions only when requested and supported by the available rubric. Keep optional polish separate from conditions for passing. End with a clear next step the student can complete.
